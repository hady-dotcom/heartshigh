# Clip Rubric v2

A clip is a 15–60 second vertical excerpt from a lecture, watched on its own.
It plays with sound on, to a viewer who did not choose it, knows nothing about
the speaker or the lecture, and will leave within two seconds if nothing holds
them.

Nothing outside the clip provides context, sets expectations, or earns
patience. Axes 1 and 2 are load-bearing: a clip that fails either cannot be
rescued by strength elsewhere.

The strongest clips have one shape: a hook that works cold, a turn (one
thought that shifts or sharpens it), and a land (the line that sticks). Judge
only the words in the clip's units. A title, summary or stated idea attached to
the clip is the clipper's claim, not evidence, and never raises a score.

## Axes

### 1. Standalone comprehensibility
Does every referent resolve inside the clip, and would a stranger take away
only what the speaker actually said?
- 1 — Opens on a pronoun, "this", "that story", or a conclusion whose premise
      was cut. Or opens on a link phrase continuing a prior thought ("as I
      mentioned", "so anyway", "as I was saying"). Or depends on the room: a
      named audience member, pointing, crowd work, pair-share, notices or
      logistics. Or, heard alone, it could be taken as a claim the speaker did
      not make (for example he voices a view he goes on to reject, and the clip
      stops before the rejection). A new viewer is lost or misled.
- 3 — Mostly self-contained; one reference requires a guess the viewer can
      make from tone.
- 5 — A viewer with no context understands the whole thing, and what they
      understand is what the speaker meant.

### 2. Hook (first 3 seconds)
Judge ONLY the words in the `hook_window` field, which is computed from audio
timings, not estimated. Does it stop a scroll?
- 1 — Throat-clearing, filler, a greeting or logistics, or a subordinate
      clause continuing a prior thought. A window that starts partway through a
      sentence belongs here.
- 3 — Clear and coherent, but no tension, question, or surprise.
- 5 — Poses a question, states a counterintuitive claim, sets up a contrast
      ("not this, but this"), or opens a story with immediate stakes.
An opening that is only recitation, or speech in a language other than the
clip's main language, with no point yet in that main language, scores no
higher than 2.

### 3. Payoff
Does the clip contain its own resolution: setup, turn and land?
- 1 — The point arrives after the clip ends, or the clip is setup with no land.
      So does a clip built around a reaction (laughter, "amen", a pause)
      when the line that earned the reaction is not inside the clip.
- 3 — Resolution is implied but not stated, or the land is stated but the
      clip trails into the start of the next sentence, list or topic. A story
      told at length where only its turn matters belongs here.
- 5 — Setup, turn and land are all inside the clip, and it ends just after the
      land sentence completes.

### 4. Quotability
Is there a line a viewer would screenshot or repeat?
- 1 — No memorable phrasing, or the key line is garbled or broken in the
      transcript so it cannot be read as said.
- 3 — One good line, buried, long, or flat.
- 5 — A sharp, self-contained line delivered with weight, usually one sentence
      of roughly 8–15 words. Strongest forms, in order: a thesis the speaker
      repeats; a verse or citation followed by a one-line application; a
      contrast; a puzzle and its solution; the turn of a story; a question
      and its answer.

## Scoring notes
- Score each axis independently. Do not average as you go.
- Cite the unit IDs that justify each score.
- A high score on one axis never compensates for a low score on another.
- Do not score the importance of the topic or the doctrine. Judge the clip as
  a standalone excerpt only.
- Most candidates are 3s. A 5 must be earned by the units you cite.
